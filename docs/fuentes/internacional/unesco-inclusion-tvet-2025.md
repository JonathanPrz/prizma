Inclusion in TVET:
Unlocking opportunity for everyone
As economies evolve and societies grow more diverse, inclusive TVET
is essential to equip all learners – regardless of ability, background or
circumstance – with the skills they need for meaningful employment and
participation in society. By integrating formal, informal and non-formal
learning, TVET helps bridge educational gaps.
Despite its potential to empower traditionally excluded groups, many
still face systemic barriers that hinder access, participation and smooth
transitions into the workforce. Globally, TVET participation remains below
5%, and a large number of youth – especially young women – are not in
```
education, employment or training (NEET).
```
TVET can close this gap by offering targeted support and skills develop-
ment, leading to improved job prospects and sustainable livelihoods.
This guide provides actionable strategies for building
inclusive TVET systems. It promotes collaboration
among NGOs, private sector actors and education
providers to ensure no learner is left behind. This
hands-on guide shows how to move beyond
identifying challenges to creating
real impact.
It offers practical tools and a five-step imple-
mentation framework, including the 4A
```
tool (Availability, Accessibility, Acceptability,
```
```
Adaptability). It proposes the use of pilot projects
```
as a start for holistic institutional transformation on
inclusion.
TVET leaders, educators and stakeholders are encouraged to
apply these approaches – starting with small-scale pilots and scaling up
strategically – to drive meaningful and lasting change in inclusion across
the sector.

TERM MEANING
Access
```
Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) opportunities are open to individuals, groups of individuals
```
and enterprises to gain knowledge and skills for work. Access determines the fairness of an education and
```
training policy and system. It can be influenced by a number of factors, including the criteria set for admission (for
```
```
example, education level); physical factors (such as the location of training institutions); economic considerations
```
```
(including the costs of education and training); social and cultural rules (for example, attitudes towards certain
```
```
population groups); and personal characteristics (such as disability).
```
Competency Competencies are knowledge, skills and attitudes acquired to achieve standard performance in the workplace.
Equality Equality is a state of affairs or a result whereby all the members of a group enjoy the same inputs, outputs oroutcomes in terms of status, rights and responsibilities.
Equity Equity is a concern with fairness, such that the education of all learners is seen as being of equal importance.
```
Exclusion Exclusion occurs when vulnerable learners cannot access or are not able to participate fully in Technical andVocational Education and Training (TVET).
```
Inclusion
Inclusion is seen as a process of addressing and responding to the diversity of needs among all learners by
increasing participation in learning, cultures and communities and reducing exclusion within and from education.
It involves changes and modifications in content, approaches, structures and strategies, reflecting a common
vision that covers all children in the appropriate age range and a conviction that it is the responsibility of the
regular system to educate all children.
Inclusive TVET
Inclusive TVET is a process to strengthen the capacity of a TVET system to reach out to all learners, building ‘a
system that meets the needs of all learners, regardless of their social background, gender, level of achievement,
ethnicity, disability, migration status, etc.’
Integration
‘Learners labelled as having “special educational needs” are placed in mainstream education settings with some
adaptations and resources, but on condition that they can fit in with pre-existing structures, attitudes and an
unaltered environment.’
Mainstreaming
Mainstreaming is the inclusion ‘of learners with special needs into general educational settings or regular schools’.
Mainstream education settings should ensure that the needs of all students are addressed and that all barriers
that can potentially hinder their participation are removed. Thus, inclusive mainstream settings should provide
inclusive and accessible school infrastructure, learning methods and pedagogies, and teaching and learning
materials, among others.
Reasonable
adjustment/
reasonable
accommodation
Reasonable adjustments are a way to remove barriers to education. Schools adjust policies and practices in
```
education and associated services for learners with disabilities. The duty applies to all staff and is anticipatory (not
```
```
compensatory). This means that adjustments are in place before learners with particular requirements are present
```
in school. It recognises each learner’s right to attend and the need to remove barriers to participation and learning.
Segregation
‘Segregation occurs when students with disabilities are educated in separate environments [classes or schools]
designed for students with impairments or with a particular impairment. Segregation is most blatant when
students with disabilities are forced to go to a school only for students with disabilities, but it also happens when
students are educated in separate classes in a regular school. These are sometimes called resource classes.’

learn, the development of literacy and numeracy skills, transversal skills and citizenship skills are integral compo-

INTRODUCTION
```
Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) is crucial for creating employment,
```
improving livelihoods and providing lifelong learning opportunities, particularly for disadvantaged
people. TVET equips individuals with practical skills, foundational knowledge and support systems
necessary to succeed in diverse industries. It also promotes innovation, creativity and critical
thinking, enhancing individuals’ resilience, adaptability, self-confidence and ability to contribute
actively to society.
Despite progress toward achieving Sustainable
```
Development Goal (SDG) 4, which promotes
```
inclusive and equitable quality education,
many individuals remain excluded. Systemic
barriers, discrimination and stereotypes prevent
equitable access and participation in TVET
programmes, often resulting in unemploy-
ment and precarious livelihoods. Addressing
these barriers is essential to ensure that TVET
contributes to economic growth and peaceful,
inclusive societies.
UNESCO’s strategy for TVET 2022–2029 prior-
itizes transforming TVET to meet current and
future challenges while promoting equitable
access and quality education for all. The strategy
focuses on three main lines of activity:
• Develop skills for all individuals to learn,
work and live: this initiative supports flex-
ible lifelong learning pathways to ensure
everyone has access to essential skills for
personal and professional development.
• Develop skills for inclusive and sustainable
```
economies: this focus emphasizes
```
equipping individuals with skills that
drive economic growth while ensuring
sustainability and inclusivity in the
workforce.
• Develop skills for inclusive and peaceful
```
societies: this effort aims to foster social
```
cohesion by providing individuals with the
skills needed to contribute positively to
their communities and society at large.
This guide reflects UNESCO’s strategic priorities
for transforming TVET for successful and just
transitions.

WHY INCLUSION IN TVET MATTERS
```
Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) refers to education designed to prepare
```
individuals for specific trades, crafts and careers across diverse industries, including entrepre-
neurship development. It encompasses education, training and skills development in multiple
```
occupational fields, such as manufacturing, services and livelihoods (UNESCO, 2013).
```
TVET is often considered an inclusive educational pathway because it creates opportunities
for groups traditionally excluded from mainstream education. Its inclusivity is enhanced by inte-
grating formal, informal and non-formal learning approaches.
Despite its potential to bridge educational gaps, TVET continues to face challenges in providing
equal access to affordable, high-quality education for all. Barriers such as poverty, geographic loca-
tion, gender, language, disability, ethnicity and migration status often limit participation.
Challenges to TVET access and participation
Global data highlights the extent of educational
```
disparities:
```
• 20% of children and youth worldwide
experience daily exclusion from education
```
(UNESCO, 2023).
```
• Nearly 40% of children lack access to
education in a language they understand.
• Youth with disabilities face disproportionate
exclusion.
• One in four young people globally were not
```
in education, employment or training (NEET)
```
in 2022, with young women facing unem-
ployment rates up to 1.5 times higher than
```
young men (ILO, 2022).
```
• In 2023, 268 million children and adolescents
in 73 countries were affected by crises, such
as conflicts or natural disasters – a 12.5%
```
increase from the previous year (Education
```
```
Cannot Wait, 2023).
```
Further, insights from the 2020 Global Education
```
Monitoring Report (UNESCO, 2020) emphasize
```
how identity, background and ability heavily
shape educational opportunities.
• In at least 20 countries, mostly in sub-Saharan
Africa, almost no poor rural young women
complete secondary school.
• Discrimination, stereotyping and stig-
matization mechanisms are similar for all
learners at risk of exclusion. While 68% of
countries define inclusive education, only
57% include multiple marginalized groups
in their definitions.
• Despite progress, many countries fail to
collect, report or use data on excluded
```
groups. Since 2015, 41% of countries (repre-
```
```
senting 13% of the global population)
```
have not conducted household surveys to
provide disaggregated education data.
• Millions are missing out on learning oppor-
tunities. In middle-income countries, despite
a 25-point increase in the past 15 years, only
75% of students are still in school by age 15.
• A major barrier to inclusion is the lack of
belief in its feasibility and value. In 2018,
one-third of teachers in 43 mostly upper-,
middle- and high-income countries reported
not adapting their teaching to students’
cultural diversity.
• While some countries are moving toward
inclusion, segregation remains common.
For students with disabilities, laws in 25%
```
of countries (over 40% in Asia and Latin
```
```
America and the Caribbean) mandate sepa-
```
rate educational settings, while only 17%
promote inclusion.
• One in four countries has affirmative action
programmes to help marginalized groups
access tertiary education.
• Teachers, teaching materials and learning
environments often overlook the value
of diversity. About 25% of teachers in 48
education systems report a high need
for professional development in teaching
students with special needs.

ideals and behaviours that characterize a school
and it is demonstrated by school leadership,
teachers, students and the larger community
```

```

interpersonal relationships and atmosphere

has a significant impact on learners’ outcomes,
including academic performance, social and
emotional development, feelings of well-being
```
and behaviour (UNESCO, 2020).
```
Stereotypes can affect students' sense of
belonging and academic achievement. Bullying
and discrimination based on ethnicity, religion,
socio-economic status or disability can lead
to social isolation and mistreatment, affecting
students' well-being and academic perform-

with reduced aggression, improved behaviour
and increased student engagement.

the values of diverse people and respecting

diverse talents and learning styles (Center for

```
Inclusion in TVET is not just an ideal; it is a
```
transformative necessity that opens doors to
quality learning opportunities for all individuals,
regardless of their background. This guide
serves as a crucial foundation for understanding
the challenges and opportunities present in
promoting inclusivity within TVET centres—
whether formal, non-formal or informal. It is
a call to action for educators, administrators
and community stakeholders to engage
in meaningful discussions, forge impactful
partnerships and implement inclusive practices
that are urgently needed in today’s diverse
educational landscape.

emphasizes intentional actions and a firm
commitment to dismantling barriers that hinder
access to education. By fostering a culture of
inclusion, we can create environments where
every learner feels valued, respected and
empowered to succeed. This guide not only
identifies what needs to be done but also
provides the practical tools and strategies

effectively. The introduction of pilot projects

foundation of TVET systems.

learners, particularly those at risk of exclusion.
